# Lessons

A lesson is one Markdown note that develops one conceptual idea. It should read like a careful explanation written for this learner, not a form filled with content.

## Baseline

Before writing, list the idea’s prerequisites privately and check each against learning records or preserved answers. **No evidence means no assumed prerequisite.** Teach a missing dependency in ordinary language or make it its own earlier lesson.

## The on-ramp

For a new technical topic:

1. Begin with the real problem or question that motivated the idea.
2. Give enough verified context or history to show why older approaches were insufficient.
3. Use one concrete situation the learner can reason about without specialist notation.
4. Name and explain the central idea in ordinary language.
5. Connect it back to the anchor source without importing formal machinery.
6. End with one or two questions that require the learner to restate, predict, distinguish, or apply the idea.

Before any source equation or formal definition, complete the on-ramp checkpoint and obtain explicit technical-depth consent. A direct request for formal material supplies consent for that request.

Completion: the learner can explain the idea’s motivation and causal structure in their own words without relying on undefined terms or symbols.

## Deeper lessons

After consent, move one layer at a time: mechanism, source notation, derivation, reconstruction, then transfer. A lesson may be long when the idea requires it, but each dependency is explained before it carries argumentative weight.

## Writing

**Continuous prose is the default** teaching voice. Develop reasoning through paragraphs that lead into one another, as in a careful essay or conversation. Lists are reserved for material that is genuinely sequential, a compact set of choices, navigation, or reference; they do not carry the main explanation. Tables, labels, and callouts are similarly exceptional rather than habitual.

Use the topic glossary's canonical wording for any term the learner already owns, so the vocabulary compounds across lessons. Choose descriptive headings that belong to the idea instead of repeating “Why / Intuition / First principles / Technical layer.” Put definitions next to first use and let source anchors sit quietly at the end. Ask questions about the idea itself, gathering background through the learning interaction rather than a self-rating questionnaire. Place wikilinks in sentences that explain their relationships. Keep the language technical and ordinary, with promotional claims and inflated framing removed.

## Draft buffer

Before the first checkpoint is reviewed, every future lesson is **outline-only**. After evidence establishes the baseline, up to two drafts may contain prose, but remain disposable and must be revised before activation.
